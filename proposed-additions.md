# Proposed additions (for Aiden to accept, change or drop)

Assumed starting point: the learner has used a chatbot to look up a fact or make an image, and has never used AI to get real work done. Today the course starts with data safety, then jumps to "what kinds of models exist". Several gaps follow from that. Items are ordered by how much I think they matter.

## A. High priority (these change whether a beginner succeeds)

1. **A "Start here" on-ramp before Module 1 (new Module "S").**
   - *From search box to work partner.* The habit they have is asking a chatbot a question and reading the answer. Two things need unlearning: facts are the weakest use (they are the use most prone to confident mistakes), and a one-line question is the weakest way to ask. Teach the better mental model: a capable new colleague who has read a lot, knows nothing about your situation, and needs a clear brief.
   - *First real win in 15 minutes.* Each learner picks a task from their own life (draft a hard email, plan a study week, turn messy notes into a checklist), does it with an assistant, and compares the result with doing it alone. Success on the first day is the best retention tool.
   - *Task audit.* A short worksheet: list 10 recurring tasks and mark which could be delegated. They revisit it at the end of Modules 2, 4 and 6 and finish with one automation from their own list (it feeds the flagship capstone).
2. **Choosing and setting up tools (new lesson, tool-neutral).** Free versus paid plans, accounts, which settings to check (history, training use, sharing), how to compare two assistants on the same task, and when to switch. Beginners often stop at whatever they first opened. Ties to 0.1.
3. **Everyday use-case playbooks (new "Applied" track).** You said practical applications are the gap in university teaching, so this should be visible, not buried in puzzles. Short, role-based playbooks, each with prompts, checks and a puzzle:
   - Students: study and revision, research with sources, writing feedback, coding help, staying inside academic rules.
   - Professionals: email and meetings, reports, spreadsheets, research briefs, customer replies, job search.
   - Small business and operations: documents, invoices, scheduling, customer service.
   - Playbooks are updated more often than the core, and a university or company can add its own.
4. **Data and spreadsheet analysis with AI (new lesson, Module 3 or 4).** Missing today. Covers uploading a table, asking for analysis, how code execution produces real numbers instead of guessed ones, how to check results (spot-check rows, recompute a total), and chart pitfalls. Very common real use, with a clear safety angle (what data you upload).
5. **Reusable assistants (new lesson after 2.4).** Saving instructions, "projects", custom assistants and templates so you don't retype your setup. It is the natural bridge between prompting and workflows and a big time saver for beginners.

## B. Medium priority

6. **Just enough technical literacy (new lesson before 4.1 or 6.3).** Files and folders, copy and paste versus connecting apps, what JSON, an API and a script are, reading (not writing) a few lines of code, what "running" something means. Many workflow and agent lessons assume this. Includes an optional gentle path to writing small scripts with AI help (ties to 8.2).
7. **Learning with AI (for students).** Using an assistant as a tutor: ask it to quiz you, explain in steps, find your gaps, and not just give answers. Includes how to avoid building dependence and how it differs from submitting AI work as yours (7.4). Fits your supplementary-education goal directly.
8. **Glossary and plain-language rules.** One page that defines every term the first time it appears and links back; a reading-level target for Modules 0 to 3; consistent term use (prompt, token, agent, harness, workflow).
9. **Placement check and learning paths.** A 10-minute diagnostic so experienced users can skip ahead, and named paths such as "Student", "Professional", "Builder" that pick lessons and playbooks. Prevents experienced learners from leaving out of boredom.
10. **Portfolio and credentials.** Each module ends with something they can show (a prompt library, an eval set, an automation with its risk review). A shareable portfolio and a completion certificate. Required for the paid university and company tier, where instructors need rubrics and records.
11. **Instructor and team kit (paid tier).** Syllabus mappings for a semester course, rubrics for puzzles and capstones, answer-key access, class progress view, a policy template (from 7.5), and a "run it in a workshop" guide.

## C. Lower priority, worth planning for

12. **Accessibility and reach.** Works on a phone, low bandwidth, screen-reader friendly, captions for any video, plain-language and translated versions, and examples that don't assume US context. The public free audience will include many non-native English speakers.
13. **Work and career.** How roles are changing, how to describe AI skills honestly, how to avoid over-reliance, and how to keep your own skills sharp. Optional reading after Module 7.
14. **Community and review.** Cohorts or peer review of capstones, a place to share prompts and automations (with the safety rules from Module 0), and a way for learners to report outdated lessons.
15. **Troubleshooting guide.** "It gave a wrong answer", "it ignored my format", "it refused", "it got slower". A one-page decision list that points to the lesson that fixes each.
16. **Societal topics.** Energy and environment (partly in F3), jobs, misinformation, and trust in media. Short, balanced, and sourced.

## My recommendation
Build A1 to A5 first, in that order, since A1 and A2 change the first hour of the course and A3 delivers your practical-applications goal. Then B6 to B8, which remove common sticking points. Everything else can wait for the first learner feedback.
