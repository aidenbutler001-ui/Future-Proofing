## Read
Automations and agents can be rolled out faster than the oversight around them. Many organizations run agents they cannot fully see. Good oversight answers five questions:

1. **What is running?** A list of every agent and automation, who owns it, and what it can access.
2. **What did it do?** Logs and traces of inputs, tool calls and outputs, kept long enough to investigate.
3. **Is it still working?** Monitoring against the quality, cost and failure measures from lessons 6.4 and 6.5.
4. **Who can stop it?** A clear off switch, and a named person who can use it.
5. **Who is accountable?** A person, not the model, answers for the result. "The AI did it" is not an answer to a customer, a professor or a regulator.

Two design principles:
- **Don't rely on one check.** A single monitor can miss things, and a system can score well on a single metric while failing the real goal. Use several independent checks and sample real outputs by hand.
- **Put humans at the right points.** Review is most valuable where decisions are costly, irreversible or uncertain. Review everything and people stop reading.

Be careful when many agents work together (lesson 5.3). Each hand-off is a place where goals can drift or problems go unnoticed, so more agents means more need for visibility, not less.

## Puzzle: Design the oversight
A simulated company runs 5 agents. You get their access lists and last month's log summaries. Choose what to monitor, set alert rules, assign owners, and place human checkpoints.
- Goal: all 4 planted incidents (a cost spike, a silent quality drop, an agent with unused write access, an unreviewed customer-facing change) are caught or prevented, with at most 10 false alerts.
- Hidden tests: 4 incidents plus a month of normal noise.
- Grader: simulator counts caught incidents and false alerts; feedback names the check that was missing.
