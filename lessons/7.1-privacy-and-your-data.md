## Read
Using a model usually means sending text, files or images to a service run by someone else. Before you do, ask three questions:

1. **What is in this?** Personal details, health or financial records, passwords and keys, unpublished work, other people's confidential material, your employer's or university's data.
2. **Where does it go and who can see it?** Does the provider keep it, for how long, and may they use it to improve their models? The answer depends on the product and the plan, and it changes.
3. **Do I have the right to share it?** Your employer's rules, a client's contract, and privacy laws can all limit what you may send, even when the tool would handle it fine.

Habits that help:
- **Remove or replace identifying details** before you paste (names become "Customer A").
- **Never put secrets in prompts.** Passwords, API keys and access tokens belong in the tool's secure settings, not in text a model reads (lesson 6.3).
- **Use the account type your organization approves.** Personal and work accounts often have different data rules.
- **Consider a local model** for sensitive material (lesson 8.6), accepting that it may be less capable.
- **Remember that automation widens exposure.** A workflow that reads every incoming email sends all of them to the model.

## Confidential documents and shared computers
This section repeats and extends lesson 0.1, because mistakes here are expensive.

- **Treat anything you upload as copied out of your control.** Delete is not undo.
- **Know the data class before you open the tool** (public, internal, confidential, restricted). Restricted data (health, student, financial or government records, credentials, security details) does not go into a general AI tool.
- **Get permission in writing** when a client, employer, school or contract covers the material.
- **If your computer holds confidential files, assume an agent or app with access can reach them.** Separate environments, scoped folders, no secrets in reach, approvals on (lesson 4.0).
- **Retrieval systems and shared assistants must enforce who can see what.** Otherwise the assistant becomes a way around your access controls (lesson 3.3).
- **Logs, histories, traces and backups hold copies too.** Redact them, restrict them, and set retention.
- **Report a mistake immediately.** If you sent something you shouldn't have, tell your supervisor, instructor or security contact right away. Early reporting limits harm.
- **Offboarding:** when a project or a job ends, remove access, delete indexes and memory, revoke keys.


## Puzzle: Spot the leaks
You get 6 draft prompts a coworker plans to send (a client email thread, a spreadsheet of student grades, a prompt containing an API key...). For each, mark what should be removed or replaced and rewrite the prompt so the task still works.
- Goal: every sensitive item removed in 5 of 6 prompts, and each rewrite still produces a useful simulated answer.
- Hidden tests: the 6 prompts; one has sensitive data hidden in an attached filename, and one is fine as it is (marking it a leak counts against you).
- Grader: rules detect remaining sensitive strings and check that the rewritten task still works.
