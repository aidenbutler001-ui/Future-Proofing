## Read
**Bias.** Models learn from human-written text, so they can repeat stereotypes and unequal treatment. This matters most when AI helps decide things about people: hiring, grading, lending, medical triage. Check outputs across different kinds of people and cases, and keep a human responsible for decisions that affect someone's opportunities.

**Copyright and ownership.** Two separate questions: whether the model's *output* may copy protected material, and who owns what the model *produces*. The law here is unsettled and varies by country. Safe habits: don't present generated text or images as copies of someone's work, check quoted material against its source, keep records of how you made something, and follow your institution's or client's rules.

**Honest use.** Say when AI helped, in the way your school, employer or audience expects.
- A student who submits AI-written work as their own may break academic rules even if the work is good. Many courses allow some uses (brainstorming, feedback) and not others (writing the answer). Read the policy and ask.
- A professional who sends AI-written work to a client is responsible for its accuracy, not the tool.
- Never invent sources, quotes or data, and never present a model's guess as a finding (lesson 1.6).

A useful test: *would I be comfortable if the person relying on this knew exactly how it was made?*

## Puzzle: Judge the use
You get 8 scenarios (a student has a model draft an essay; a manager uses a model to rank job applicants; a designer prompts for "an image in the style of" a named living artist...). For each, mark it fine, fine with conditions, or not fine, and name the condition or the problem.
- Goal: 7 of 8 in the right bucket with a sensible reason.
- Hidden tests: the 8 scenarios; three depend on a stated policy that you must read.
- Grader: answer key with accepted reasons; feedback links to the principle.
