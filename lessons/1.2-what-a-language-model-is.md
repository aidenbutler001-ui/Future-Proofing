## Read
A language model (LLM) is a program trained on a very large amount of text. Given some text, it predicts what text should come next, one small piece at a time. Chains of those predictions look like answers, essays and code.

Three consequences matter every day:

1. **It aims for plausible, not true.** A fluent answer is not evidence of a correct one. The model can state a wrong fact in the same calm tone as a right one.
2. **It only knows what it was trained on, plus what you show it.** Training stops at a cutoff date. Newer events are unknown to it unless the tool gives it a way to look things up.
3. **Wording changes the result.** The same question phrased two ways can get two different answers. That is not a glitch. It is how the system works, and it is why prompting is a skill.

An assistant like a chatbot is the model plus extra software around it: a system prompt, sometimes search or file tools, and a chat window. When we say "the AI did X", usually the whole bundle did X.

## Example
Ask a model: "What was the score of the game last night?" With no search tool it cannot know. A well-behaved assistant says so. A poorly behaved one invents a score. Both answers are fluent. Only one is useful.

## Puzzle: Predict the failure
You get 6 short questions and a model's answers (pre-written). For each, choose what went wrong or "nothing":
- invented fact, outdated fact, wrong arithmetic, ignored instruction, or fine.

Goal: 5 of 6 correct.
- Hidden tests: the 6 question and answer pairs. Include one arithmetic slip, one post-cutoff event, one fabricated quote.
- Grader: exact match against an answer key; feedback names the idea ("this answer sounds sure but there was no way to know").
