## Read
New models, tools and claims appear every week. You can't follow all of it and don't need to. A routine:

**1. Choose a few sources and a fixed time.** Two or three reliable sources (a lab or tool's own release notes, a trusted newsletter, a field-specific community) and a weekly or monthly slot. Ignore the rest.

**2. Sort what you read.**
- **Capability claims** ("it can now do X").
- **Price or speed changes.**
- **Standards and policy changes** (a protocol, a law, a platform rule).
- **Safety and security findings.**
- **Marketing.**

**3. Ask four questions of any new thing:**
- What can it do that I couldn't do before?
- What does it cost per task?
- How does it fail, and who says so (the maker, or someone independent)?
- What would I have to change to use it?

**4. Check claims at the source.** Find the primary page: the release notes, the paper, the specification, the official pricing page. Be careful with roundups that disagree with each other, with numbers quoted from other articles, and with a single source making an extraordinary claim. Impressive results in a demo often don't hold in your workflow. A claim of a scientific discovery that experts dispute is a live example of lesson 1.6: don't repeat it until it is independently confirmed.

**5. Test on your own evals** (lesson 5.5). A small set of your real tasks beats any benchmark for deciding whether something helps you.

**6. Adopt slowly.** Trial on low-stakes work, compare, then widen. Re-run your tests after any model update (lesson 6.5).

**7. Keep a changelog** of what you adopted, when and why. It is the memory your future self needs.

**How this course keeps up:** Module 8 is reviewed every quarter. Each lesson has a review date, a "Right now" note for time-sensitive material, and an owner. A review checks claims against primary sources, reruns puzzles on current models, and adds or retires topics.

## Example
An announcement says a new model "doubles agent reliability". You find the release notes, see that the test was on tasks unlike yours, run your own 10-task eval, find a modest gain at higher cost per task, and decide to use it only for the two tasks that improved.

## Puzzle: Evaluate the announcement
You read a (simulated) product announcement with five claims. List what you would verify and where, design a 5-case test, and decide: adopt, trial or ignore.
- Goal: identify the claims that need checking, name the right primary source for each, and design a test that could actually show a difference.
- Hidden tests: the announcement contains one claim that cannot be tested and one that contradicts the product's own documentation.
- Grader: rubric on claim sorting, source choice and test quality; feedback highlights what a skeptic would ask.

## Hints
1. Which claim, if false, would change your decision?
2. What would count as a fair test on your own work?
