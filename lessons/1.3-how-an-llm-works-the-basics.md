## Read
No math needed. Here is the whole idea in four steps.

**1. Text becomes tokens.** The model can't read words directly. Text is split into **tokens**, pieces roughly three quarters of a word long. "Lighthouse" might be two tokens; "the" is one.

**2. The model scores what comes next.** Given the tokens so far, the model gives every possible next token a probability. After "The capital of France is", the token " Paris" scores very high.

**3. One token is chosen, then the loop repeats.** The chosen token is added to the text, and the model scores the next one. A full reply is just this loop run hundreds of times. A setting called **temperature** controls how adventurous the choice is: low means mostly the top-scoring token (steady, repeatable), high means more variety (creative, less predictable).

**4. The scores come from training.** Before you ever use it, the model was shown huge amounts of text and repeatedly adjusted so its predictions got closer to what the text actually said next. This is **pretraining**. It is where the model picks up grammar, facts, styles and some reasoning patterns. A second stage, often called **fine-tuning** or **alignment**, shapes it to follow instructions and behave helpfully and safely.

What the loop explains:
- Why it can be fluent and wrong: it is choosing likely text.
- Why the same prompt can give different answers: there is a random choice in step 3.
- Why wording matters: the earlier tokens change every later score.
- Why it has a cutoff date: training ended at some point.

What it doesn't mean: the model is not looking things up in a database, and it is not a person. Newer tools add search, calculators and other tools around the loop. You'll meet those in Module 4.

## Example
Prompt: "Once upon a". The model scores " time" at about 99%. At temperature 0 you always get " time". At a high temperature you will occasionally get a surprising word.

## Puzzle: Be the next-token predictor
A simulated model shows a sentence stopping mid-way, with five candidate next tokens and hidden probabilities.
- Part A: rank the candidates by how likely you think the model finds them. You see the real ranking afterward.
- Part B: a temperature slider changes which token gets picked across 10 runs; set it so the output is nearly identical each run, then so it varies.

- Goal: Part A ranks the top candidate correctly in 4 of 5 sentences; Part B settings land in the right ranges.
- Grader: rules. Compare against the stored probabilities; check the slider ranges.

## Hints
1. Think about what usually follows. Common phrases score high.
2. Low temperature narrows the choices.
