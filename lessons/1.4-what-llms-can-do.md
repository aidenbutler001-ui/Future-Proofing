## Read
LLMs turn out to be useful for a surprising range of text-shaped work. Groups of tasks they handle well:

- **Transform:** summarize, rewrite, translate, change tone, reformat.
- **Generate:** draft emails, outlines, code, ideas, examples.
- **Extract:** pull names, dates, amounts or themes out of messy text.
- **Classify:** sort messages, tag items, spot sentiment.
- **Explain:** break down a concept at the level you ask for.
- **Converse:** act as a tutor, practice partner or sounding board.

Usual weak spots, and why (from lessons 1.2 and 1.3):
- **Exact facts and citations** (plausible, not verified).
- **Precise arithmetic and counting** (the model predicts text; calculators compute).
- **Anything after its cutoff**, unless given a search tool.
- **Knowing its own limits.** It may not tell you when it is guessing.
- **Long tasks done in one shot.** Errors compound without checking steps.

A rule of thumb: LLMs are strong where *a reasonable draft is valuable and you can review it*, and risky where *a wrong answer is expensive and hard to notice*.

The edge moves fast. Tools that let a model run code, search, or use software turn many "can't" items into "can, with help". That's the subject of Module 4.

## Puzzle: Green, yellow, red
You get 10 tasks (e.g. "draft a polite decline", "give the exact population of Lisbon", "turn a table of notes into bullets"). Sort each into green (good fit), yellow (use with checking) or red (use a different tool).

- Goal: 8 of 10 in the same bucket as the key, with none of the "red" tasks put in green.
- Hidden tests: the 10 tasks; a few depend on whether a tool such as search is available (the scenario says so).
- Grader: answer key; feedback links each to the cause (verification, math, cutoff).
