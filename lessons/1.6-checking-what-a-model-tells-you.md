## Read
You can't trust output just because it reads well. You also can't check everything. The skill is checking in proportion to the stakes.

A simple routine:
1. **Sort the claims.** Which are facts you could be wrong about (dates, numbers, quotes, names, citations) and which are reasoning or style?
2. **Check the ones that carry weight.** Open the source. If a model cites a paper, find the paper. If it quotes someone, find the quote.
3. **Ask the model to show its basis.** "What in the document supports this? Quote it." A quote you can find in the text is checkable. A claim with no quote is a flag.
4. **Match effort to risk.** A brainstormed list needs a glance. A figure going into a contract needs a source.

Fabricated references are a known failure: a citation can look perfect (authors, journal, year) and not exist.

## Example
A model summarizes a contract and says "Clause 7 caps liability at $50,000." You search the document for Clause 7. If the cap is $500,000, a fluent summary just cost you.

**Opsec check.** Checking a claim means opening sources. Don't paste a sensitive document into a search tool or a second AI to "verify" it. Check against the original instead (lesson 0.1).

## Puzzle: Catch the made-up source
You get a model-written paragraph with five references and a mock library to look them up. Each lookup returns real or not found, and some real sources don't support the claim.

- Goal: flag all fabricated references and all real sources that don't support the claim, with at most one false alarm.
- Hidden tests: two paragraph sets with different mixes.
- Grader: answer key. Feedback explains which check would have caught each.

## Hints
1. A real source can still be misquoted. Read what it says.
2. Start with the claim that, if wrong, would hurt most.
